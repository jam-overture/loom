## What the lesson is actually about, which is not frames

Lesson 19 closed by handing the reader a pattern and calling it a shape rather
than a coincidence:

> a name in the tree, an address in a registry, resolved before the walk

Two seams fit it. **The third breaks two of the three clauses**, and that is the
lesson. A frame's URL stays in the tree; nothing is resolved before the walk;
only the registry clause survives, and it survives holding something other than
an address.

I want to be plain that this was not a device I imposed on the material. I went
in expecting to teach an allowlist and found that the interesting thing in 0095
is what it does to the previous lesson. Writing the third instance of a pattern
is where you find out which clause was doing the work — for a form's
destination, "the address goes in the registry" and "the part that does not vary
goes in the registry" are the same sentence, and the frame is the case that
separates them. *Which* video is content and varies per revision; *whose*
documents a deployment will run inside its own pages is a deployment fact that
is the same on Tuesday as on Monday.

So Predict 1 asks the reader to apply lesson 19's answer directly — register the
embed URLs — and then asks what happens on the day somebody uploads a video. The
design looks fine; the operations are what break it. That is a better way to
find out a pattern has a boundary than being told it has one.

Two consequences I leaned on hard because they are the ones a reader would
otherwise take on faith:

- **The two-state verdict falls out of the first decision.** A submission has
  three states because a tree that never named a destination is a distinct
  fault. Keeping the URL in the props is what removes that state — a node either
  carries a URL or renders no frame. The reader is asked to derive the second
  decision from the first rather than to memorise both.
- **No plan and no resolve is a property of allowlists, not a shortcut.** Data
  and submissions need three steps because a host adapter may query anything and
  a target is minted per request. An allowlist is a static list and a URL parse,
  so it runs inside the walk. Set X question 3 says "both Part V seams resolve
  in three steps", which was true when it was written and is now true of two out
  of three — Set Y question 1 is where that gets picked up.

## The half nobody finds on their own

Predict 2 is the question I would keep if I could keep only one:

```html
<iframe src="https://app.example.com/preview/42"
        sandbox="allow-scripts allow-same-origin"></iframe>
```

served from `https://app.example.com`. What does the sandbox protect the page
from? **Nothing.** `allow-scripts` beside `allow-same-origin` is a boundary only
because the framed document is cross-origin; between an origin and itself it
hands the framed document full access to the page that framed it.

That is a fact about browsers rather than about Loom, and it is the reason the
`self` flag exists. It also produces the one genuinely strange thing in the
renderer, which Exercise D shows in a line of output: `frame-same-origin` is
**the only diagnostic raised for something that worked.** I checked this rather
than taking 0095's word for it — all eighteen codes in
`src/render/diagnostics.ts` name a failure except that one.

The reason it is a disclosure rather than a refusal is the part worth arguing
with, and Self-check 5 makes the reader argue both halves. A host that
registered its own origin and marked it `self` said what it meant, and a seam
that refuses what a deployment explicitly permitted gets worked around. But this
is the only place in the system that *can* notice — the primitive cannot, the
tree cannot, the URL does not say — and noticing silently is the same as not
noticing.

## What the exercises turned up

All six executed from the fences as published: I extracted the seven `ts` fences
out of the finished markdown, concatenated them the way `exerciseProgram` does,
ran them, and pasted back what came out. Two produced something I had not
planned for.

**Exercise B — a URL that comes back changed.** The tree says
`HTTPS://PLAYER.VIMEO.COM:443/video/76979871` and the `src` the primitive places
is `https://player.vimeo.com/video/76979871`. I had written the exercise to
demonstrate refusals and the normalisation is the better half of it: the scheme
and host lowercased, the default port dropped. That is the difference between a
check and a suggestion, and it is visible rather than asserted, so the exercise
now asks the reader to predict the exact `src` string for each allowed URL
rather than just allowed-or-refused.

**Exercise B again — `/embed/local` is refused, and lesson 19 accepts it.** A
same-origin path is exactly what `isSameOriginPath` permits for a form action.
The same shape of string, two seams, opposite answers, and the reason is good: a
form action is resolved by the browser against the page it is on, and a frame
has to be checked against an allowlist of origins *before* anything resolves, so
a relative path has no origin to check yet. This is the sharpest discrimination
point in Part V and I did not go looking for it — it fell out of putting a
plausible string in a list.

**Exercise E — the case that registers happily.** I wrote it to show the
registry refusing `frames: ["source"]` after a prop rename. The third case,
`frames: []`, registers and checks nothing, because an empty declaration is what
almost every primitive in the library correctly says. So the guard rail catches
the drift that leaves evidence and misses the drift that removes it — and this
seam is the one that **fails open**, where a drifted declaration means the
allowlist silently stops applying to a URL that still reaches an `iframe`. The
lesson keeps that as a limit to sit with rather than a footnote, because 0095
says the same thing in its own consequences and it is more convincing as
output.

## Found while teaching

**Two, both for `Loom daily build`, both filed in `FINDINGS.md`.**

**1. The frame seam cites 0094 eight times and means 0095.** Every doc comment in
`src/frame/`, `src/render/` and `src/sdk/` that names its own decision record
names the wrong one — `index.ts:7`, `origin.ts:143`, `request.ts:71`,
`render.ts:104`, `primitive.ts:110`, `registry.ts:62`, `audit.ts:48`,
`definition.ts:41`. 0094 is "A card's prose is a child when the card has a
flow" — a real Accepted record about something else.

This is **the same failure yesterday's run filed against 0087**, one day later
and eight times over. Yesterday's finding closed by suggesting a check that a
citation inside a record resolves; this is the same check wanted one level down,
over `src/`, and eight sites in one seam is a better argument for it than one
site was. Two things stop it being a find-and-replace: the three citations of
0094 in `src/primitives/library.test.ts` are **correct**, and
`loom.embed.ts` — a different lane — cites 0095 correctly throughout, which
suggests the seam's comments were written before the record was numbered.

**2. 0095 says no primitive uses the seam, and one has since 26 August.** The
record's Consequences say `loom.embed` "still reads its own `src` prop" and that
"this record describes machinery with no consumer." Both are false:
`loom.embed.ts` declares `frames: ["src"]`, reads `loom.frames["src"]`, places
`framed.url` rather than `given.src`, and renders a notice on refusal.

This is [0099](../../decisions/0099-a-record-is-amended-when-only-the-count-moved.md)
territory rather than a supersession — the decision stands entirely and a fact
underneath it moved. It is worth more than a stale count, and that is the
argument I would make if anyone weighs the two: 0095 names this as a **cost it is
paying** and a reason to be uncertain about the interface. Somebody deciding
whether to build on the seam reads "no consumer, ergonomics untested" and weighs
it, when the honest state is the opposite. A stale count misinforms; a stale
consequence changes a decision somebody is about to make.

I did not fix either. `src/` and `decisions/` are not this lane's, and a lessons
pull request that also changes behaviour is one nobody can review.
