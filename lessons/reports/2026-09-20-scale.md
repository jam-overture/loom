## Which lesson, and why this one was next

Part V's tenth seam, and the settled subject with the most left to teach:
[0150](../../decisions/0150-a-container-that-must-aggregate-publishes-a-custom-property-and-the-browser-does-the-arithmetic.md),
`Accepted`, dated 13 September and landed on the 14th. Seven days old, one
primitive pair, one composition, no open pull request touching any of it.

It earns a lesson rather than a paragraph because it is **the odd seam in Part
V**, and that is the whole reason to write it. Every earlier one is a fact Loom
cannot reach: a registry somebody else fills, a host page the runtime never sees,
a party who has not declared, a compiler answering a narrower question, a second
store the first may not open. This one is six numbers in one array, one level
below the node that needs them, in the same render, in front of the same reader —
and the container still cannot have it, because a render is a total pure
projection of **one node** and a container receives its children *already drawn*.

So Part V gains a kind it did not have: not a fact out of reach, but a fact
inside reach that a promise forbids. The remedy is correspondingly different —
not *reach further* but *hand the work to a different machine* — and that turned
out to be the transferable thing on the page.

## What I emphasised, and why

**The line of type, not the principle.** The lesson opens The problem by quoting
`LoomPrimitiveProps` and pointing at `children: ReactNode` — *"Rendered children
in tree order."* Almost every reader who has written React carries a picture in
which a parent maps over its children's props, and that picture is correct in
React and wrong here. Predict 1 asks for the route the number takes and then
asks, if the answer was *the container maps over its children*, **what the
container was holding when it mapped**. The failure is productive because it is
specific and it is one line away from being fixed.

**What you inherit when you move a computation.** The section I would keep if I
could keep one, and it is not about CSS. Hand a computation to another machine
and you take on that machine's data model; its limits become limits of your
design. Custom properties carry *a scalar*, *by inheritance*, so every child gets
the same one — which is exactly right for a ceiling and forbids a rank, an index,
a running total, and *draw the tallest one differently*. That limit is not a
limit of Loom or of 0008; it arrived attached to the solution. Predict 3 asks the
reader to derive it from the channel's shape before meeting it.

**And then the aggregate is not computed.** The turn the lesson exists for.
Follow the argument and you expect it to end with the browser working out the
maximum; it does not, and cannot — inheritance runs downwards. The browser does
the *division*. The *aggregation* is performed by nobody, and the reason is not
mechanical: where an axis stops is an argument the author is making. A chart that
picked its own ceiling would make the same argument every time — the tallest bar
full height in every chart — so a series that crept from 94% to 96% and one that
tripled both reach the top, and the top of the plot stops meaning anything
outside the band you are looking at.

The sequence the lesson leaves the reader with: *the renderer may not compute it*
→ *find a machine that may* → *and then decline to, because it was never a
measurement.* The first two steps are engineering and the third decides what the
primitive is.

**Checking versus delivering.** Explain-it-back 2 asks the reader to derive this
lesson's shape from 21 and 22 — a property belonging to no single party, a fault
belonging to two nodes — and find that neither answer applies. The distinction is
between a fact somebody has to **check** and a fact somebody has to **deliver**,
and Part V had only ever met the first. The prompt says explicitly that the hard
half is what *follows* from the difference, because an answer that stops at
"there is nothing to check" has stopped one sentence early.

**The clamp as a two-axis judgment.** `min(1, max(0, …))` lives in the rule. The
lesson makes the reader weigh it the way lesson 08 weighs a change: a bar drawn
out through the band above is a **broken page** with no diagnosis available from
looking at it; a bar at full height beside a printed figure larger than the axis
is **merely scaled wrong**, and a reader can see that and roughly why. Exercise C
prints the second at a ceiling of 60, and five flat bars is an unmistakable
shape.

## What the exercises revealed

Seven exercises, every transcript generated by running the program. `run.test.ts`
compiles and executes all seven against this checkout's `src/`, and
`transcripts.test.ts` holds every line of every fence against what they printed —
both green, and the pinned recognised-transcript count moved 95 → **102**,
exactly seven.

**Exercise D is the lesson, and it produced a finding.** Four quarters of revenue
in millions, plotted with no `max`:

```
  render diagnostics: 0
  the chart's own props: {"outcome":"valid"}
    Q1 {"outcome":"valid"}
  ...
  analysis ok? true
  stakes: medium  gate: accepted

  and what the page will draw:
    Q1  $0.8M    0.8%
    Q2  $1.2M    1.2%
    Q3  $1.9M    1.9%
    Q4  $3.4M    3.4%
```

Every instrument in the repository says yes. Nothing is failing; nothing is
looking. I did not expect the Gate line — I expected to write *and the Gate is
the wrong instrument for this* and move on, and building the delta to check took
four minutes and turned an assertion into a printed `accepted`. That is the whole
argument for the exercise discipline in one paragraph.

**Exercise B is byte comparison rather than assertion, and it is better for it.**
One `loom.stat` inside a chart and the identical node inside a grid, compared as
strings: `identical? true`. The child does not know which container it is in and
has no route to find out; one draws a column and the other draws a figure in a
row, and the entire difference is an ancestor's class and a rule matching on it.
Saying "renders identically" is a claim. Printing the two lines is a fact, and it
is the property React context would spend.

**Exercise E started as a footnote and found something.** I added it to print the
rule and noticed the two `var()` fallbacks. Constructing the markup that would
fire either one: `var(--loom-chart-max, 100)` needs a chart that published no
ceiling, and the component writes `given.max ?? 100` unconditionally — rendered
a chart with no props at all and it still carries one. `var(--loom-stat-magnitude,
0)` needs a `.loom-stat-plotted` with no magnitude, and the class and the property
are set in the same ternary. **Neither can fire.** The third property in the same
rule carries no fallback and is exactly as reachable, which is the tell.

**Exercise F found lesson 24's distinction dying at the glass.** A month measured
at `0` and a month nobody measured are unmistakable in the markup — one carries
`loom-stat-plotted` and a custom property, the other carries neither — and
identical on the screen, because a zero-height bar and no bar look the same.
The reading is honest and the rendering is not, and the rendering is the part the
reader gets. That is not filed as a defect because a fix is a design question for
the primitive's owner, but it is taught, and it is the sharpest instance of
lesson 24's rule this course has met outside lesson 24.

**Exercise G is the fourth sighting of ids running the other way.** Six stats
build to `n_1`–`n_6`, their chart is `n_7`, the page is `n_8` — children before
parents, so the root has the highest id in the tree. It costs one line in the
lesson and it is lesson 04 arriving unbidden for the fourth time, which is what
interleaving is supposed to feel like.

## Found while teaching

**Two findings, both for `Loom primitives`, neither fixed here.**

1. **The ceiling is argued to be the author's and implemented as a default.**
   0150 and `loom.stat-chart.ts` both say being forced to state the ceiling is a
   feature. Nobody is forced: `max` is optional, the component defaults it to
   `100`, and the catalogue tells a model `props: max?, plot?`. The default is
   right for the one series whose ceiling is not a matter of opinion and silently
   wrong for every other, and exercise D is what that costs. The entry names the
   remedy the record's own argument implies — a required `max` — with its real
   price, and explicitly declines to ask for a heuristic diagnostic, on lesson
   25's grounds.

2. **Two loose clauses in 0150's supporting material**, neither changing
   behaviour and neither touching the conclusion, which is right. The unreachable
   fallbacks above, where `100` now sits in two files with only one copy readable
   and nothing holding them together. And the argument against React context,
   which rejects it because it would break rendering a node alone — *"which the
   portal does when it previews one"*, present tense. The portal does not: it
   renders the whole tree and decorates the selected node. `renderLoomExcerpt`
   exists, was added on 14 September, and **is called by no surface in this
   repository.** The decision is still right and this lesson defends it; naming
   `renderLoomExcerpt` rather than the portal costs two words and is true.

Filed rather than fixed: `src/primitives/` is another lane's, and a lessons
branch that changes behaviour is a lessons branch nobody can review.
