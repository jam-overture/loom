## What I emphasised, and why

The 08 report asked for an angle beyond "here are the six rules in order", and
suggested precedence as an authored artefact. That is the spine, and three
choices came out of building around it.

**The ladder is observable from outside, so teach it that way.** Exercise B takes
one change that trips everything, then relaxes exactly one knob at a time and
prints seven verdicts. The seven reason codes come out in ladder order, and the
reader has derived `ESCALATION_RULES` without opening `gate.ts`. Presenting the
array first and then illustrating it would have taught the same six words and a
much weaker thing, because the exercise makes the order *falsifiable*: predict
row 4, run it, find out.

The row that earns the exercise is not any single verdict, it is what is true
across rows 2–6. Confidence sits at 0.5 against a minimum of 0.7 on all five, so
rung 6 is true five times and named once. That is the honest cost of
first-match-wins, and it is not in any decision record: **a count of reason codes
over a corpus is a count of *first* reasons, and each rule is undercounted by
however often something above it also fired.** The size of the error is a
property of the policy, not of the model, so tightening a refusal floor makes an
apparent confidence problem improve. The lesson states it plainly, gives the
mitigation (the assessment holds every factor unranked; the disposition is a
decision, not the evidence), and does not pretend the cost is zero.

**The confidence pair is the best available proof that position is authored.**
One number — `provenance.confidence` — is read by rung 1 and by rung 6, the two
ends of the ladder. If a reader believes position follows from which input a rule
reads, that pair refutes it in one line: position follows from what a rule
*concludes*, and these two conclude opposite things. It also makes Self-check 2
and Set K question 1 possible without any code in front of the reader.

**`confirmDiscardsLaterWork` is the case where a value cannot do a rule's job.**
Exercise C is the one I would keep if I had to cut five: two changes, both `high`
stakes, both from a developer, one accepted and one held. The level is identical,
so the level cannot be what decided. The third row runs it again with
`autoApplyCeiling: { developer: "critical" }` to close off "the ceiling in
disguise". The general statement — *a level cannot express "never auto-apply",
because the ceilings are per origin* — is the sharpest thing in the lesson and it
falls straight out of two lines of output.

Deliberately left thin: the pipeline. The lesson never says where an assessment
comes from or what happens to a `requires-confirmation` afterwards. That is 10,
and Set K question 8 is the only place I let attribution reach forward at all.

## What the exercises revealed

All six were executed via `src/scratch.test.ts` and every output in the lesson is
a real one. Two exercises changed what the lesson claims.

**`critical` is refused under every policy that can be written.** I set out to
write exercise D as an origin sweep and found the ceiling has a hard top:
`refusalFloor` cannot exceed `critical` because `critical` is the top of
`STAKE_ORDER`, and rung 2 tests `isAtLeast`. So a critical change never reaches
rung 5, and — executed — `autoApplyCeiling: { developer: "critical" }` and
`{ developer: "high" }` produce identical verdicts on both a `high` change and a
`critical` one. **`autoApplyCeiling` has three behaviourally distinct values, not
four.** A host writing `critical` there believes it is saying something.

**The reachability test from lesson 08 transfers to the ladder, and finds
something.** Lesson 08 asked whether every combination of two axes is reachable;
pointed at a ladder the question becomes *can this rule fire at all*, and under
some policies it cannot. Both findings below came out of that, and the second
one came out of checking whether the first generalised — which it does, exactly.

The lesson presents this as an open question with both cases argued, so it stays
correct whichever way the two findings are resolved.

**A correction to lessons 07 and 08.** `stakes.ts` has *eight* factor codes, not
seven — `protected-type-relocated` was added by 0044 on 2026-08-08, the day
before lesson 08 was written, and the count was not updated. Lesson 08's "In the
code" table and, worse, its one-week review item ("write the seven stake factor
codes") both said seven, which would have had the reader stop one short and
conclude they had recalled everything. Lesson 07's hypothetical ("seven rules
walk the tree seven times") had the same stale number. Three words, fixed in this
PR; no exercise or argument depended on the count. Worth noting the mechanism,
since it will recur: a lesson that names a *count* of anything acquires a
maintenance burden the rest of the prose does not have.

## Found while teaching

**Three items, all for the build routine. Nothing was fixed here, and none of the
three is a behaviour bug.**

**1. `gatePolicySchema` accepts `confidenceFloor > minimumConfidence`, which
deletes rung 6.** The two knobs sit on one scale and the schema validates each
independently as a number in [0, 1], never comparing them. Executed, floor 0.9
against minimum 0.5, beside a policy with both at 0.9:

| Confidence | `inverted` (0.9 / 0.5) | `collapsed` (0.9 / 0.9) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` |
| 0.7 | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` |
| 0.89 | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` | `rejected` / `confidence-below-floor` |
| 0.9 | `accepted` / `within-policy` | `accepted` / `within-policy` |

Not merely a quiet rule: the inverted policy **is** the collapsed one, on every
input. `confidence-below-minimum` requires a confidence at or above 0.9 and below
0.5. The host's `minimumConfidence: 0.5` reads like a setting in a config file
and reaches nothing.

**My recommendation: no `superRefine`; say it in the schema comments.** A
two-outcome gate — *refuse below 0.9, never merely confirm* — is a coherent thing
to want, and refusing to parse the policy would replace a host's explicit
configuration with the runtime's opinion about what a host may mean, which is the
trade 08 declined over overlapping vocabularies. What is not defensible is the
current middle: the host writes a number, the runtime accepts it, and it means
nothing. Two lines next to the pair, noting that the minimum is inert unless it
exceeds the floor, put the fact where someone setting them will be looking.

**2. The same shape on the stakes scale: a `refusalFloor` at or below an origin's
ceiling makes `confirmAboveCeiling` inert for that origin.** Executed, one
`medium` change from `developer` under `refusalFloor: "medium"`, with all four
ceilings:

| `autoApplyCeiling.developer` | disposition | reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `low` | `rejected` | `stakes-at-refusal-floor` |
| `medium` | `rejected` | `stakes-at-refusal-floor` |
| `high` | `rejected` | `stakes-at-refusal-floor` |
| `critical` | `rejected` | `stakes-at-refusal-floor` |

The generalisation, which the lesson makes the reader derive rather than read:
under this floor the only level that reaches rung 5 is `low`, and `low` is at or
below every ceiling, so no setting of the knob changes any verdict for that
origin. Combined with the top-of-scale finding above, `autoApplyCeiling` is a
four-valued knob with three meanings under the default floor and one meaning
under a floor of `medium`.

**My recommendation: document, do not validate.** Same argument as item 1, and
the two must be answered together — they have the same shape, so validating the
confidence pair while leaving the stakes knobs alone needs a reason for the
difference and I do not think there is one. A sentence on `refusalFloor` saying
it is consulted before `autoApplyCeiling` and therefore bounds what any ceiling
can express is the whole fix.

**3. Reason codes are first-reasons, and anything that aggregates them inherits
that.** Nothing today aggregates by reason code — 0047's calibration report
partitions by `policyId`, not by reason — so this is a note for whoever writes the
next reader rather than a defect in an existing one. If a `byReason` breakdown is
ever added to telemetry, it will systematically undercount every rule by however
often a rule above it fired on the same change, and the undercount moves when a
host edits a policy. Executed in exercise B: five consecutive verdicts where the
minimum-confidence rule was true, one that says so.

**My recommendation: nothing now; a line in `disposition.ts` on
`dispositionReasonCodeSchema`** saying the code is the first rule that fired and
not the only one that could have. That is cheap, and it is the sentence that
stops someone from writing `countBy(reason.code)` and presenting the result as a
fact about the interpreter.
