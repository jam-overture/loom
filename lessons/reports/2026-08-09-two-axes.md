## What I emphasised, and why

The syllabus line is "the distinction that a test caught us getting wrong", and
the temptation is to teach that as an anecdote at the end. It is the spine
instead, for a reason worth writing down: **the bug ran toward caution.** The
July implementation refused a change it should have offered to a person. Nobody
files a bug because the system asked permission, so a design error in that
direction survives review indefinitely — it took a test that had committed to an
expected disposition in advance. That is a better argument for writing the
expected verdict down than any amount of prose about testing.

Three choices about how to build the lesson around it:

**A criterion, not a slogan.** "Two independent axes" is the kind of phrase a
reader can repeat without being able to use, and the usual gloss — the axes read
disjoint inputs — is *false here*. Two of `ChangeAnalysis`'s ten fields feed both
axes: `removedNodeCount` (via `large-removal` and via the retention budget) and
`touchedPrimitiveTypes` (via `protected-type-touched` and via
`out-of-tree-effect`). So the lesson replaces the slogan with something testable:

> Two axes are two axes when every combination of their values is reachable.

That is empirical, it is exercise C, and it makes the 2026-07-28 bug diagnosable
rather than merely regrettable — one cell was unreachable, and the unreachable
cell was a change the system could not describe. It also survives the discovery
that a shared input exists, which the disjointness gloss does not.

**The boolean is a claim about the world, the inverse is a claim about the
tree.** `reversible: false` arriving with a complete, working inverse attached is
the most counterintuitive thing in this material, and it is where lesson 06's
argument against snapshots finally pays: a snapshot can only ever answer the
tree-level question, so it always answers yes. Exercise B takes an inverse from
an assessment that has just declared the change irreversible, applies it, and
compares roots. Reading that argument is much weaker than watching it print
`true`.

**`discards-later-work` as the boundary case.** The clearest statement of where
the line between the two axes falls is not on either axis — it is the thing that
could plausibly have been irreversibility and is damage, because the discarded
revisions stay in the log and undoing the undo restores them. Set J question 4
and Self-check 4 both target it, and Self-check 4 asks what would have to change
for the answer to flip, which is where 0037's forgetting journal gets named
without being explained.

Deliberately left thin: the rule ladder. Exercises E and F call `gate` and print
dispositions, and the lesson states only the two orderings the two axes force
(irreversibility before the ceiling; the refusal floor above both). Everything
else is 09.

## What the exercises revealed

All six were executed via `src/scratch.test.ts` and every output in the lesson is
a real one. Three were worth more than expected.

**Exercise A inverts the reader's ranking.** Deleting a whole slot trips three
`medium` factors and comes out `medium`; setting one prop the host called
precious trips one factor and comes out `high`. `highestStake` is a maximum, so
factor count never enters the level. Predicting that in writing first is the
whole value — "three concerns beat one" is a strong intuition and the output is
two lines.

**Exercise D found a real gap, and the interesting part was the fix's cost.** I
built it to show the retention budget working and it showed the budget's unit
instead. Under a 1-node budget: a 4187-byte inverse is refused, a 4075-byte
inverse is waved through, because the second destroys no *nodes* — it overwrites
two 2000-character props, and the old values ride into the inverse as `set`
values that `retainedNodeCount` does not count. Third run running where executing
an exercise changed the lesson rather than confirming it.

**The grid in exercise C is the load-bearing one.** One delta with
`removedNodeCount: 2`, four policies, all four cells of low/medium ×
reversible/irreversible, with `retained` sitting at 2 in every row because it is
that same number. Nothing else in the lesson makes "shared input, independent
axes" as concrete, and the fifth row pins the budget comparison as strictly
greater — a removal that exactly fills the budget is reversible.

Also worth recording: `pnpm verify` now exits 0 from a clean clone in this
environment (886 + 227 tests), so item 1 of the 2026-08-04 report — typecheck
failing before `build` had produced `dist/` — is resolved by `ce06ffc`.

## Found while teaching

**Three items, all for the build routine. Nothing was fixed here, and none of the
three is a behavior bug.**

**1. `retainedNodeCount` is `removedNodeCount`, and telemetry keeps both.**
`assessReversibility` assigns `retainedNodeCount: analysis.removedNodeCount`
verbatim — the reversibility axis computes no size of its own. Both land in
`assessmentSummarySchema` and both are written per change, so every row of the
corpus carries the same integer under two names. Not wrong today, and it is the
sort of thing that stops being harmless quietly: the two columns are provably
equal, so a query may be written against either, and the day the definitions
diverge every one of those queries keeps running and starts meaning something
else.

**My recommendation: change nothing, say why.** A line on the field noting that
it is the removed-node count under a different name, and that a future measure of
the inverse's true weight should be a *new* field rather than a redefinition of
this one. That last clause is the part with value.

**2. `inverseRetentionBudget` counts nodes, so it cannot see content destroyed by
`configure`.** Executed, one policy at `inverseRetentionBudget: 1`, against a card
carrying two 2000-character props:

| Delta | `retained` | inverse bytes | `reversible` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `remove` the card | 2 | 4187 | `false` |
| `configure` both props to `""` | 0 | 4075 | `true` |

The refused inverse and the accepted one differ by under 3%. A `configure`
destroys the previous prop values — nothing in the tree holds them afterwards and
the inverse is their only home — but they are not nodes, so the check that reads
`removedNodeCount` contributes zero for them, always, regardless of size.

The field names are honest: `inverseRetentionBudget` is documented as "nodes an
inverse delta may retain", which is exactly what it does. The gap is between that
and the stated purpose, "before undo stops being practical", since practicality
is about weight.

**My recommendation: document, do not change behavior.** Two lines in
`policy.ts` saying the budget is denominated in destroyed nodes and therefore
does not bound an inverse's serialised size. Whether the gap is worth closing
depends entirely on whether hosts put content in props, which is a question about
deployments rather than about Loom, and closing it means item 1's new field
rather than a redefinition. The lesson teaches it as an open question with both
cases argued, so it stays correct whichever way this goes.

**3. 0002's "no input may feed both axes" holds over declarations, not over
fields or types.** The record states it as an invariant of the design; the code
satisfies it by giving each axis its own policy vocabulary, not by keeping the
measured inputs disjoint. Two analysis fields feed both axes today, which is fine
by the reachability criterion — and a host that lists one primitive type in both
`protectedPrimitiveTypes` and `outOfTreeEffectTypes` makes a cell unreachable
again. Executed, on a `configure` of a card, `refusalFloor: "high"`:

| Policy | stakes | reversible | disposition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `outOfTreeEffectTypes` only | `low` | `false` | `requires-confirmation` (irreversible) |
| both lists | `high` | `false` | `rejected` (stakes-at-refusal-floor) |

That is 0002's July symptom — small, confident, permanent change refused rather
than escalated — reached by configuration rather than by the runtime. I do not
think it is a defect. A checkout genuinely is both precious and out-of-tree,
0002 explicitly tells hosts to compose knobs for a hard block, and `policyId` is
stamped on the disposition so the record says which policy did it.

**My recommendation: do not enforce disjointness; narrow the wording.** A
sentence in `gatePolicySchema`'s comments noting that a type appearing in both
vocabularies moves both axes, and — if it is cheap — the same qualification in
0002, because a reader of that record currently has grounds to believe the
runtime prevents something it does not. Refusing overlapping policies would
replace a host's explicit configuration with the runtime's opinion about what a
host may mean, which is a worse trade than the ambiguity.
