## The lesson

Part V asks what a tree cannot hold. The first four seams answered it the same
way — a name in the tree, a document in a registry, resolved before the walk —
and lesson 22 broke the pattern by having a fact that belongs to two nodes and
no document anywhere. Its closing instruction was to look for another fact that
exists only between two things.

An anchor is one, and it is the case where **both ends are in the tree**. The
page names the slug and the page names the link. Nothing is registered by
anybody, nothing is resolved before the walk, and the reason it is still a Part
V seam is new: the *scope* the name has to be unique in is bigger than anything
the system can see. An `id` is unique within a document, and the document
includes a host page the runtime has never been shown.

What I emphasised, in order of how much I would defend it:

1. **Three questions arrive with naming a place, and a props schema can answer
   exactly one of them.** Is it validated, may two nodes share one, does a copy
   carry it. The first is what a schema is for. The second is a fact about a
   *pair* and the validating function has never seen the other node. The third
   is a fact about something that is not in the tree at all — a decorative copy
   is made by the render (0093). This is the whole argument for the reserved
   key, and it is an argument about the **unit of judgement** rather than about
   anchors.
2. **The grammar is a claim about a round trip, not about HTML.** An `id` may be
   nearly anything since HTML5. What the grammar protects is the journey from
   the tree, through a URL somebody copies out of an address bar, into a
   message, and back to the element. Capitals fail because fragment matching is
   case-sensitive and `Pricing`/`#pricing` scrolls nowhere *silently*.
3. **First claim in document order, and the ordering is a fact about the tree
   because `renderElement` was arranged to make it one** — the anchor is
   resolved above the line that renders the body. Get those two lines the other
   way round and "first" becomes a fact about the renderer's recursion.
4. **The primitive gets the attributes or nothing**, where lesson 20's frame
   seam hands over a refusal the primitive can render. The difference is not
   carelessness: there is no way to draw a refused `id`, and a shape offering a
   choice nobody can act on invites one to be invented.
5. **The limit is stated rather than fixed.** A prefix would have closed the
   host-page collision and broken the tree's ability to name its own
   destination. 0098 writes the hole down instead, and it is the first Part V
   seam that cannot use the escape the other four use — you cannot ask a
   deployment to enumerate the ids in its own static HTML and keep that list
   true.

Exercise C is the one I would keep if I could keep one. Three nodes name
`pricing`; the winner is `n_3` and the node it beat is `n_2`, because the
fixture builds children before parents and document order is an ancestor before
its descendants. That is lesson 04's *an id says nothing about position*
arriving inverted — the one time the reading is tempting is the one time it is
backwards — and it came out of the run rather than out of a plan for it.

## What the exercises found, and it is the mirror of last time

Exercise F asks the starter library the questions exercises A to E asked three
hand-written primitives. Against `main`:

```
  loom:anchor, the reserved key
    ids:         []
    diagnostics: none
  anchor, the prop
    ids:         ["pricing"]
    diagnostics: none
  the prop, on two bands
    ids:         ["pricing","pricing"]
    diagnostics: none
  primitives registered: 91
  declaring an "anchor" prop: loom.section, loom.hero, loom.callout
  "loom:anchor" anywhere in the catalogue: false
```

**Not one of the ninety-one primitives spreads `loom.anchor`.** The runtime
checks the slug, claims it in the ledger, builds the attribute bundle and offers
it; the section drops it; the page has no anchor and no diagnostic, because
nothing the render seam can see went wrong. Meanwhile the `anchor` prop — the
alternative 0098 examined and rejected by name, shipped in the same pull request
as the seam that replaced it — is what actually emits an `id`, and two bands
carrying it produce two identical `id="pricing"` with nothing reported. That is
precisely the document the record exists to prevent, reached by the route it
said would reach it.

Filed for `Loom primitives`, with the two-line assertion that would have failed
the day it shipped. Not fixed here.

It is worth naming why neither lane could have caught it. `anchor.test.ts`
asserts that a primitive which spreads `loom.anchor` gets an `id`.
`library.test.ts` asserts the prop emits one. **Both suites pass, both describe
a system that works, and neither can see that they are not describing the same
system** — because no assertion anywhere ranges over the library and asks
whether any registered primitive places what the runtime hands it.

This is the mirror image of lesson 22's finding rather than a repeat of it.
That one was a check firing on a page that was fine; this one is a check firing
on nothing at all, and the second is the quieter failure. A false alarm gets
filed by whoever it annoys. An unreachable check gets filed by nobody, and this
one had a fortnight.

The second finding is one character: `src/reserved-props.ts:28` cites 0096 for
the anchor key, where the record is 0098. Filed for the framework lane.

## The exercise discipline, again

Two things the run got wrong before it printed anything, both caught by running
rather than by reading:

- A `configure` operation in exercise G was written as `{ op, nodeId, props }`.
  The shape is `set` and `unset`, and `analyzeDelta` threw on the missing
  object rather than reporting anything — a scratch-file error, not a defect,
  but exactly the kind of thing a lesson would otherwise print as though it
  were a finding.
- `loom:submit` was written as a bare endpoint id. It is `{ to: … }`, so the
  redirect comparison in exercise G reported nothing at all, and for about a
  minute I had what looked like a second finding — *a moved form destination
  isn't detected* — which would have been wrong and loud. `declaration.ts` is
  where the shape is, and the corrected run produces the `redirected-submission`
  factor the record describes.

I also wrote exercise D's commentary around a transcript I had not re-run, and
caught it when checking the printed lines against the saved run: every plain
fence in Try it was compared line by line against the real output before
committing, and the Try it program is byte-identical to the file that produced
them. 65 transcript lines across 7 blocks.

## What is next

Part V has six lessons, and the three-clause pattern is now the minority
reading. The question to carry into a seventh is this lesson's rather than
lesson 22's: **what is the scope of the thing you just named, and is the checker
allowed to see all of it?** Every seam so far has an answer, and this is the
first where the honest answer is no.

finding is acted on, exercise F's transcript and the paragraphs under it go
